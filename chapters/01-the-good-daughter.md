## ◆ i. The half-second before yes

*Eleanor*

The morning she turned thirty-four, Eleanor Hale woke before the alarm and lay very still, listening to Adam breathe, doing the thing she had done every morning of the four years they had shared a bed, which was to wait until she could tell from the rhythm of him whether he wanted to be woken or left alone, and then to be, accordingly, the version of herself that the rhythm asked for.

He wanted, this morning, to be left alone. His breathing had the long, untroubled draw of a man with another forty minutes in him, and so she did not move, did not let the duvet sigh, kept her own breath shallow and patient and turned only her eyes to the window, where the curtain they had never quite finished hanging let in a grey wedge of Bristol light. Somewhere below the hill a gull was being unreasonable about something. The radiator ticked as it warmed, three times, the way it always did, a little phrase she could have set to music if she had ever learned, properly, to do that — her mother's gift, skipped a generation, or refused by one.

Thirty-four. She tried the number on the way she tried, in changing rooms, the clothes she would not buy. It fit. That was the trouble with all of it lately; everything fit, nothing was hers, and she had got so good at the small adjustments — taking in a seam here, letting out a hope there — that she could no longer have told you, if you had woken her in the dark and asked, what size she actually was.

She thought, *I am thirty-four years old and I am very good at lying still.*

It was not, she would want it understood, an unhappy thought. Eleanor was not an unhappy woman. She had been told all her life that she was easy — *such an easy child, you were, never any trouble, you'd play for hours with a saucepan and a wooden spoon and not a peep* — and she had grown into an easy adult, the friend who would always shift the booking, take the middle seat, eat the end of the loaf, the colleague who could be relied upon to have no strong feeling about the thing everyone else had strong feelings about, and she had built, on this foundation of easiness, a life that was, by every external measure, sound. A flat she half-owned with a man who loved her. A career. A mother she visited. A best friend. A body that did, on the whole, what she asked of it. Doors, she sometimes thought, on the architectural side of her brain — she had a great many doors, and she kept every one of them open, because an open door was a possibility and a closed door was a loss, and she had a horror, the precise dimensions of which she had never measured, of loss.

The alarm went at seven. Adam surfaced the way he did everything, considerately, with a small apologetic groan that took responsibility for the noise on the clock's behalf, and reached across her to silence it, and then, remembering, kissed the top of her head.

"Happy birthday, Nell."

"Thank you."

"Thirty-four." He said it with satisfaction, as though it were an achievement they had managed together, which in a sense it was; they had managed everything together, smoothly, for four years, the way two careful people will. "How does it feel?"

"Like thirty-three with worse knees."

He laughed, which she had counted on; she was, among her other easinesses, reliably and minorly funny, the kind of funny that asked nothing of a room. Adam got up. She listened to him in the bathroom — the tap, the particular two-note creak of the door he kept meaning to plane, the engineer in him forever cataloguing the small failures of the building and forever, like her, deferring the repair — and she made, lying there, the first decision of her thirty-fourth year, which was about tea.

He would make tea. He always made tea on her birthday; it was one of the rituals by which Adam, who was not a demonstrative man, demonstrated. And she would drink it, or she would mean to, and then her mother would ring, or her phone would, or she would start tidying the thought of the day into order, and the tea would go cold on the bedside table with its single sip taken out of it like a bite from an apple in a painting, and Adam would find it there at lunchtime and tip it, without comment, down the sink. This had happened, she estimated, something on the order of two hundred times. He had never once said anything. It was, she thought, one of the kindest things about him and one of the loneliest things about them, that he had simply absorbed her wastefulness with tea into his sense of who she was, a small known fact, *Nell lets her tea go cold,* filed alongside her shoe size and her dislike of coriander, and had stopped, years ago, expecting her to drink it.

She decided, this morning, to drink the tea.

It was the sort of resolution that on any other day she would not have dignified with the word, and she did not, even now, entirely trust where it had come from — some thirty-fourth-birthday instinct toward the keeping of small promises to oneself, perhaps, or just the gull, still going on outside about its grievance. But she held on to it as she got up, as she stood at the basin and looked at her own face in the mirror Adam had wiped a porthole into the steam of — thirty-four, the same face, the eyes her mother's, the mouth her father's, that she knew chiefly from photographs and could not look at without the small administrative ache of an account left permanently, deliberately, unsettled. *Drink the tea,* she told the face. The face looked unconvinced. She had, she noticed, the face of a woman who agreed with everyone.

She went down through the flat to the kitchen, and as she went she did the thing she always did, the thing she had not known she did until Adam, early on, had pointed it out, half charmed and half something else: she opened the doors. Not unlocked — opened. The flat had four internal doors that closed, and Eleanor could not be in a space with a closed door for long without easing it open, the bedroom, the bathroom, the spare room they called the study and used as the place coats went to die, the cupboard under the stairs; she liked to be able to see through a building, all the way through, liked the sightline that ran from the front window to the back, liked a home you could move through without ever having to put your hand to a handle and *decide* to go in. Adam, who closed doors — who closed them firmly, who liked a room to be a room, who had grown up one of five and understood a closed door as a small mercy you granted other people — had said, in the first month, *you leave every door open in the place, it's like living in a show home, like nobody actually lives anywhere,* and had meant it lightly, and Eleanor had laughed and not been able, for some reason, to laugh it all the way off, because he had put his finger, without meaning to, on the floor plan of her whole life. *Like nobody actually lives anywhere.* She had thought about it for days. She still thought about it. She went on, every morning, opening the doors.

They had been four years in the flat, which they had bought at the exact midpoint of the relationship, in the way of careful people, as a sort of structural test — *if this holds, we'll know* — and it had held, of course it had held, two people as accommodating as Eleanor and Adam could make almost anything hold; the danger was never that their building would fall down, the danger, if it was a danger, was that you could not always tell, from inside, whether a structure was standing because it was sound or because nothing had ever once leaned on it. Adam had not proposed in those four years and had been, for the last of them, visibly about to, and Eleanor had developed, in response, a whole silent architecture of avoidance and accommodation, a way of being lovely and present and slightly, untouchably elsewhere whenever the jeweller's quarter came up, a way of keeping the door to that particular room open — always open, never entered — so that Adam could stand on the threshold of it as long as he liked and she would never have to say *come in* and never have to say *don't.* It was not cruel. She would have been horrified to learn it was cruel. It was the kindest thing she knew how to do, which was to give a person unlimited access to the outside of a decision.

He called her Nell. Almost everyone called her Nell; she had been Nell since school, since Priya, Nell that fit in a single warm syllable like a coin in a slot. Her mother called her Eleanor, the whole grave length of it, and only sometimes, only when it mattered, the rest of the time *darling* or nothing at all. And once, long ago, a man had called her El, just the breath at the front of the name, *El,* as though the rest of it were understood between them and did not need saying — but that was a door she kept not open but closed, the one closed door in the whole flat of her, closed and not quite locked, and she went past it every morning the way you go past a room where someone is sleeping, quietly, without turning the handle, telling yourself it is kindness and not fear.

Down in the kitchen Adam had the radio on low and was buttering toast with the focused competence he brought to all small tasks, a man who had never in his life done anything by halves except, possibly, propose to her, which he had been not-quite-doing now for the better part of a year, in the form of significant pauses and unaccountable trips to "look at something" in the jeweller's quarter and a way of saying *when we're married* and then catching himself, and she had let him not-quite-do it, had kept that door, too, propped courteously open, neither walking through it nor closing it, because to close it would have been a loss and to walk through it would have been a choice, and Eleanor did not, if she could possibly avoid it, choose.

"Tea's there," he said, nodding at the mug, the good mug, the one with the kingfisher. "I did it strong. Birthday strength."

"Thank you." She picked it up. It was exactly too hot to drink, as it always was, the heat a kind of postponement built into the gift itself, and she stood there holding it and feeling its warmth come through into her palms, her hands that — she had thought this before, she thought it again now, turning the mug — had never decided anything. They were good hands. They could draw a true line freehand and hold a level steady and play, badly, the right hand of three or four pieces her mother had despaired of teaching her. They were thirty-four years old and they had drawn a hundred buildings and signed a mortgage and held, at funerals and weddings and once, memorably, in a hospital corridor at four in the morning, other people's hands, and Eleanor looked at them and could not have named a single thing they had ever closed around and refused to let anyone take.

She lifted the mug. She blew across it. She was going to drink the tea.

The phone rang.

It was her mother's ringtone — Eleanor had given Diana her own ringtone years ago, a few descending notes, on the theory that you should always know, before you answered, whether you were about to be a daughter — and she set the mug down, the good mug, on the counter, the full mug, with its single skin of cooling at the surface, and answered, and was a daughter.

"Mum."

"Eleanor." Her mother's voice had the bright, slightly forward quality it got on the phone, the telephone voice of a woman who had taught two generations of Bristol children to count the beats out loud. "Eleanor, I've made you a cake."

"You have?" Eleanor turned her back on the tea. "Mum, you didn't have to—"

"It's a Victoria sponge. Your father's favourite." A small pause, the size of a dropped stitch. "I mean your — well. It's a sponge. Will you come for it? Today?"

"It's my birthday, Mum. Of course I'll come."

"Is it?" said Diana, and there it was, the thing Eleanor had been not-quite-noticing for the better part of a year, the way she had been not-quite-noticing the proposal, the small slack moment in the line where her mother reached for the date and found it had been moved. "Is it your birthday? Well." The brightness recovered itself, came back up like a hand finding the melody again after a stumble. "Then it's a very good thing I made a cake."

"It is. It's a very good thing." Eleanor kept her voice level, easy, the voice that cost nothing and kept the room comfortable, and across the kitchen Adam looked up from his toast and made, with his eyebrows, the question, and she made, with her own, the answer, *it's fine, she's fine,* the marital semaphore of two people who had agreed, without ever discussing it, that Diana was fine. "I'll come at four. After work. Is that all right?"

"After work," her mother agreed, satisfied, as though this had been her plan all along, and perhaps in the version of the morning that her mother was living in, it had. "Bring the young man. What's his name."

"Adam, Mum."

"Adam." A small triumphant beat, the date recovered, the name recovered, the morning back in tune. "Such a nice boy. So easy."

When Eleanor hung up, the tea had gone past drinking. She poured it down the sink herself, this time, before Adam could, and rinsed the kingfisher mug and set it on the rack, and if Adam noticed that she had not drunk it, he did not say, because that was the kindest and loneliest thing about him, and they finished getting ready for work in the comfortable, total quiet of two people who loved each other and did not, particularly, need to talk.

## ◆ ii. Load-bearing

*Eleanor*

Crane & Soder occupied the second and third floors of a building near Temple Meads that had been, in the nineteenth century, something to do with leather, and was now, in the way of such buildings, exposed brick and reclaimed bulbs and a kitchenette where the milk was a perennial source of low-grade institutional grievance. Eleanor had worked there for nine years. She had come in as the bright one, the one whose final-year project — a community library that folded around an old plane tree, that let the tree be the thing the building was *for* — had been photographed for a magazine, and she had stayed as the safe one, the one you gave the supermarket, the one you gave the care home, the one whose drawings always, always stood up.

The care home was on her screen when she sat down. Greenbank — a hundred and twenty beds on a brownfield site out past Eastville, commissioned by an operator whose other homes Eleanor had visited, dutifully, during the bid, and had come away from with a specific grey feeling she had learned not to examine too closely, a feeling about corridors, about the particular width a corridor became when its only job was to get a wheelchair from a bedroom to a dining room and back, a corridor with no idea in it, a corridor that had given up. She had drawn, for Greenbank, in the first flush of the commission, a building with a courtyard you could see from every room, a building that knew where the south was, a building with a wandering path for the residents who wandered, because some of them would, because some of them did, because — she had said this in the meeting, and watched it land wrong — *because we are designing for people who are losing the plot of their own lives, and the least we can do is give them a building that still has one.*

That had been eighteen months ago. The courtyard had survived value engineering by three weeks. The wandering path had been swapped for a longer dayroom. The building that knew where south was now faced, for reasons of plot ratio, resolutely east, and Eleanor's job this morning, on her thirty-fourth birthday, was to redraw the window schedule for the east elevation so that the rooms which would now never see the afternoon at least met, on paper, the regulatory minimum for daylight, a minimum that existed, she happened to know, precisely because if you did not legislate for light, people would build for profit a building with no idea in it, and she was the person they had hired to make sure that the building with no idea in it had, at least, its papers in order.

She remembered the meeting where the courtyard had gone. She remembered it the way she remembered all the small surrenders, with a clarity the triumphs never had — the boardroom on the top floor, the operator's man with his spreadsheet open on the big screen, the line items going down in red one after another like a tide going out, and Soder beside her doing the thing Soder did, the small regretful spread of the hands that meant *we are all reasonable people here.* The courtyard was a line item. The wandering path was a line item. And Eleanor had heard herself speak, once, into the reasonable room — had said the thing about people losing the plot of their own lives, had said *the path isn't a frill, it's the whole argument of the building, it's the difference between a place that contains people and a place that's for them* — and had watched it land wrong, had watched the operator's man receive it with the patient, almost tender condescension of a man who has heard architects be passionate before and has filleted their passion into his spreadsheet many times and found that it makes no difference to the numbers and very little to the people, who will fill the beds regardless, because their families have run out of other doors. *I hear you,* he had said, which is what people say when they have heard you and are going to proceed exactly as if they had not. *I hear you, Eleanor, and in an ideal world. But the funding model is what it is.*

And she had folded. That was the part she kept. Not the cut — the cut was his, the cut was the funding model's, the cut was the world's — but the fold, the way she had felt the heat come up into her face and had not let it reach her voice, had said, easily, reasonably, *of course, let me take it away and see what we can save,* and had taken it away, and had saved, in the end, almost nothing, and had drawn the longer dayroom beautifully, and had gone home that night and not told Adam, because there was nothing to tell, because nothing had happened, because a meeting had simply confirmed that Eleanor Hale was very good at the part of her job that came after the part she'd trained for, the part where you take the building that had an idea in it and quietly, competently, draw the idea back out, like a splinter, so that nothing is left in the flesh of it to fester or to feel. She was, Soder told clients, a safe pair of hands. It was the highest praise the building had to offer and she had spent nine years earning it and she could not, sitting at her desk on her thirty-fourth birthday, remember the last time a pair of safe hands had built anything anyone loved.

"You look very thoughtful for someone with no actual thoughts left to think about that elevation," said Cora, putting a coffee down beside her — not for her; Cora's, set there while Cora leaned over to look at the screen, the way she did, close, proprietary, already half running the project in her head. Cora Vance was thirty-one and wanted Eleanor's job, or rather wanted the job Eleanor's job was supposed to lead to, the one above it, the partnership, and she wanted it nakedly, gloriously, the way Eleanor had wanted things at thirty-one and had trained herself, since, out of. "Happy birthday, by the way. Reception's got a card going round. I signed it 'from your work wife,' which I now realise commits me to a level of involvement in your life I'm not prepared to honour."

"Noted. I'll downgrade you to work acquaintance."

"Work colleague at most." Cora tapped the screen, the east elevation, the rooms that would never see the afternoon. "You know they're going to fillet this too, when the contractor's numbers come back. The whole top floor. Watch."

"They're not going to take the top floor."

"They're going to take the top floor and you're going to redraw it for them and you're going to do it beautifully, because that's the tragedy of you, Nell." Cora said it lightly, the way you can say a true and cruel thing to someone who you are confident will not hold it against you, because they never hold anything against you, because they are easy. "You do the best work in this building and you only ever do it on buildings that don't deserve you."

Eleanor laughed, because she was reliably and minorly funny and because the alternative was to feel the thing the sentence was reaching for, the grey corridor feeling, the feeling about ideas given up. "Some of us," she said, "have rent."

"Some of us," Cora agreed, straightening, taking her coffee back, "have rent. And some of us have rent and a plan." She tapped her own temple, not unkindly, and went back to her desk, and Eleanor sat for a moment with her hands on the keyboard, her hands that had never decided anything, and looked at the east elevation, and found the load-bearing wall.

She did it without thinking; it was the oldest reflex she had. In any building, on any drawing, her eye went first to the wall that was holding the rest of it up, the line you could not move without the whole thing coming down — and it had occurred to her, more than once, in the slow grey middle of a working afternoon, that she had organised her entire life as though it had no load-bearing wall at all, as though she were a building cleverly designed so that any wall could be removed at any time without consequence, so that nothing was structural, so that she could be opened up, reconfigured, value-engineered, *adapted,* indefinitely and forever, and never come down — and that this, which she had always told herself was freedom, which she had always called keeping her options open, was in fact the opposite, was a kind of building you were not allowed to call a home, because nothing in it was being asked to hold anything up.

She redrew the window schedule. She did it beautifully. It took until lunch.

## ◆ iii. The friend who looks

*Eleanor*

Priya was already at the table when Eleanor got to the café — Priya was always already at the table, having raised the being-early of a woman with two small children to a kind of discipline, the early arrival being the only forty minutes of her week that belonged to no one but herself, and she guarded them jealously, and she had spent today's, Eleanor could see, on a flat white and the school marking she had brought in a tote bag and was not doing. They had been meeting in this café, more or less, since they were eleven; it had been three different cafés and one of them had been a McDonald's, but the institution was continuous, the oldest standing structure in Eleanor's life apart from her mother's house, and the rule of it was simple and had never been written down: here, and only here, you told the truth.

"Don't," said Priya, before Eleanor had even sat. "Don't do the face. I know it's your birthday and I know you're about to tell me not to make a fuss, and I'm telling you now, pre-emptively, that I have made a fuss, the fuss is made, it's in my bag, and you're going to open it and you're going to be moved." She pushed across a small parcel wrapped in paper her daughter had clearly decorated. "Aanya did the wrapping. There are, I'm contractually obliged to tell you, forty-one stickers on it, and she will ask you how many of them you noticed, so count as you go."

It was a fountain pen. A real one, a good one, the kind Eleanor used for the hand-drawn perspectives she almost never let herself do any more, and she turned it over in her fingers, the weight of it, and felt her throat do something inconvenient, because Priya was the one person alive who still bought Eleanor presents for the architect she had been at twenty-two rather than the one she had agreed to become.

"You shouldn't have," Eleanor said.

"I absolutely should have, and I will not be drawn into the ritual where you tell me I shouldn't and I tell you I should until we've both lost the will to live. It's a pen. Use it on something that isn't a window schedule." Priya looked at her over the flat white, sharp, fond, tired in a way Eleanor had been clocking for a few months now and filing, like everything, under *not yet.* "How's Diana?"

"She made me two cakes."

"Two—" Priya set the cup down. "Oh, Nell."

"She made one, forgot, made another. They were both there on the good plates when I—" Eleanor heard her own voice doing the thing, the easy thing, the offering-it-up-as-anecdote thing, the same move she'd made with Adam at dinner before dinner had even happened, and she watched Priya decline to take it, watched her oldest friend simply sit there and not laugh and not smooth it over, and felt, under the table, her own hands go still. "It's getting faster," she said, in a different voice. "The forgetting. I went to the GP with her about it in the spring and they did a memory test and she got eight out of ten and charmed the doctor and we all went home, and I think — I think I've spent six months letting her charm me too. Because it's easier. Because she's so good at making it look like nothing that I get to pretend it's nothing."

"That's not you letting her," said Priya. "That's her protecting you. It's the same thing she's always done and it's the same thing you've always done back, the pair of you, this — " she made a gesture, two hands smoothing an invisible cloth, " — this folding the bad thing up small enough that nobody has to look at it. You learned it from her. You're very, very good at it." She said it without unkindness, the way she said everything, a teacher delivering a hard mark to a pupil she believed in. "It's going to cost you, one of these days, being this good at it."

Eleanor looked at the pen. "How's Dev?"

And there — she saw it, the small flat moment in Priya's line, the dropped stitch, the place where the tune stepped on something — there was the thing Priya had brought today and not yet put on the table, and Eleanor, who could read a silence the way she could read a load path, understood that her oldest friend had arrived early not only for the forty minutes but because the forty minutes at home had got harder to come by.

"Dev's been offered a thing in Manchester," Priya said, after a moment, turning her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, the way Diana turned on the piano stool. "A good thing. A running-his-own-department thing. The thing he gave up, when we — when Aanya, when we decided I'd be the one who—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "We've been married nine years and I can count on one hand the number of things he's actually wanted for himself in that time, because he's been holding the other end of the thing I wanted, which was here, which was the kids in this school and my mum round the corner and the whole — the life. The life we chose. And now there's Manchester, and he hasn't said he wants it, he's far too decent to say he wants it, but I can feel him wanting it, across the kitchen, at night, the way you can feel weather coming." She looked up. "And the awful thing, Nell, the thing I can't say to anyone but you in this stupid café, is that part of me is furious at him for wanting something, after all this time. As though wanting were a betrayal. As though the deal was that we'd both stop."

"That's not awful."

"It's a bit awful."

"It's a bit awful," Eleanor agreed, and they almost smiled, and didn't. Outside the window the lunchtime crowd went by under their umbrellas, all those people walking quickly toward lives they had presumably chosen, and Eleanor thought about the load-bearing wall, the one you cannot move without the whole thing coming down, and understood that this was what Priya had built, exactly this — a wall, a real one, a marriage and two children and a mother round the corner and a job and a postcode, an entire structure in which almost nothing could be moved without consequence, in which Priya could not simply be opened up and reconfigured and value-engineered and adapted, because she had let things become load-bearing, had let herself want a life so specific that wanting anything else now arrived as weather, as betrayal, as Manchester — and that this, this terrible unfree specific costly thing, was the difference between Priya's life and Eleanor's, and that Eleanor had spent fifteen years quietly congratulating herself on being the free one.

"Can I say something," said Priya, "that I've been wanting to say since roughly 2009."

"You're going to anyway."

"I'm going to anyway. It's your birthday, you can't hit me." Priya leaned in. "You are the most capable person I know and I have no idea what you want. None. I've known you since we were eleven and I could tell you what your mum wants and what Adam wants and what that terrifying child Cora at your work wants, and I have watched you give every single one of them a hand getting it, and if you asked me right now, on pain of death, to tell you one thing — one — that Eleanor Hale wants, just for herself, that nobody else gets anything out of, I would fail. I would die. And I think the really frightening thing, the thing I lie awake about sometimes, on the nights I'm not lying awake about Dev, is that I don't think you could tell me either."

It landed in the exact place the *easy one* had landed in her mother's kitchen, the same flattened note, the same small sweet wound. Eleanor opened her mouth — and felt, distinctly, the half-second arrive, the readiness, the deflection already loaded, *I want a quiet life, I want you to be happy, I want my mother to stop making two cakes,* all of them true and all of them about somebody else — and for once, in the oldest standing structure in her life, in the one place the rule was the truth, she did not fire it. She let the half-second pass. She let Priya watch her not have an answer.

"I don't know," Eleanor said. "I genuinely don't know. Isn't that — " she laughed, and it came out wrong, came out as the thing it was, " — isn't that a thing to find out at thirty-four."

Priya reached across the marking and the wrapping paper and the forty-one stickers and took her hand, the way Diana had taken her wrist, except that Priya was not holding on to be reassured; Priya was holding on to say *I'm here when you do find out, and it might be terrible, and I'll still be here.* "You've got time," she said. "You've got loads of time."

She was wrong about that, as it turned out, in a way neither of them could have known, sitting in the café with the rain going by. Eleanor had, from the following morning, exactly three hundred and sixty-five days.

## ◆ iv. The flat D

*Eleanor*

Her mother's house stood near the top of one of the streets that went up out of the harbour at an angle the council had long ago given up signposting as anything other than unsuitable for the infirm, a tall thin terrace the colour of clotted cream gone slightly off, three windows wide and four storeys up, with a front room that had been, for thirty years, a place where children came to be afraid of a metronome.

Eleanor let herself in with her own key. The hall smelled the way it had always smelled, of beeswax and cold radiator and, underneath, very faintly now, of something she had started to notice on her visits this past year and had filed, like the proposal, like the dropped dates, under *not yet,* a smell that was not dirt exactly but the absence of the particular vigilance that keeps a house from becoming a museum of itself. There was post on the mat that should not still have been on the mat. There was a cardigan on the newel post that Diana had been looking for, Eleanor happened to know, for three weeks, and had asked her to look for, twice, having forgotten in the interval both the asking and that she had hung it there herself.

"Mum?"

"In here." The front room. And then, before Eleanor reached the door, the sound that the house was really made of, the sound that was underneath the beeswax and the cold: the piano. Three notes, a pause, three notes — and then the phrase, slow, deliberate, the left hand walking patiently up under the right, the Gymnopédie, the first one, the one that everybody knew and her mother played as though no one had ever known it, as though she were finding it, note by note, for the first time, in the wood. Diana played it the way she had played everything for the last forty years, beautifully and with one wrong note, the D above middle C that had gone flat sometime in the nineteen-nineties and that Diana had refused, on principle, to have corrected — *it's honest, that note,* she used to tell her pupils, *everything else in this room is pretending, but that note tells the truth* — so that the piece arrived, each time, at the same small sweet wound, the same flattened place where the tune stepped on something, and went on.

Eleanor stood in the doorway and listened to her mother play, and did not go in until it was finished, because some things you do not interrupt.

"There you are," said Diana, lifting her hands from the keys, and turned on the stool, and for a moment — Eleanor saw it and her heart did the thing it had been learning to do this year, the small dropped beat, the flat D of it — for a moment her mother looked at her with the open, unplaced courtesy of a woman receiving a caller she has not yet identified, a pleasant stranger, *a daughter, perhaps,* and then the recognition came up under it the way the left hand comes up under the right, and Diana said, "Eleanor. My Eleanor. Happy birthday, darling," and held out her arms, and Eleanor crossed the room and bent into them and was, for the length of the embrace, nine years old and entirely safe, and smelled the powder and the wool and underneath it the new smell, the *not yet* smell, and held her mother's small straight back and felt how much smaller it had got.

"You played my piece," Eleanor said, into her mother's hair.

"Did I? I don't know what I play any more. My hands play and I just—" Diana let her go, sat back, looked at her own hands in her lap with mild, courteous surprise, as though they belonged to a pupil who was coming along better than expected. "I just sit here and let them. It's the only part of me that still knows what it's doing." She said it lightly, the way Eleanor would have said it, the way Eleanor said everything, and Eleanor understood, not for the first time but more completely than before, exactly where she had learned to make a hard thing sound easy. "Now. There's cake. I made you a cake."

The cake was on the kitchen table. It was a Victoria sponge, and it was, Eleanor saw, two cakes, or rather it was one cake made twice, because her mother had made it that morning, forgotten making it, and made it again, so that there were two sponges side by side on two of the good plates, one slightly more risen than the other, both dusted with sugar, both waiting, and between them the bag of flour still out, and the scales, and two cracked eggs' worth of shell in the compost caddy and then, a few inches away, two more.

"I may," Diana admitted, following Eleanor's gaze, "have got a bit ahead of myself."

"They look perfect," Eleanor said. "Both of them. We'll have one now and you'll keep one." She was already moving, already easy, already the daughter who took the middle seat — putting the kettle on, finding the cake forks, clearing the doubled shells into the caddy, restoring the kitchen to a version of itself that had only made one cake, smoothing the morning out the way she smoothed all mornings, so that when Adam came at four, and when, later, they all sat down, there would be no evidence, no two cakes, no flat moment in the line, nothing but a mother who had made her daughter a birthday sponge, which was true, which was the truest thing in the house, and which Eleanor was abridging, even as she preserved it, into something easier to hold.

"You were always like this," Diana said, watching her tidy. "Even tiny. You'd come into a room and put it right. Other children made the mess; you went round behind them folding it up." She said it fondly, proudly, the old refrain, the foundation Eleanor had been built on. "So easy. I used to thank God for you. After your father went — I used to think, well, at least I was given the easy one."

Eleanor's hands went still on the cake fork.

It was the kind of thing her mother had said a hundred times, the kind of thing that had always landed as love, that *was* love, she did not doubt it was love — and yet standing there in the kitchen with the doubled cake and the cardigan on the newel post and the new smell in the house and the whole careful structure of her thirty-fourth birthday around her, Eleanor heard it, for the first time, as the sentence it also was. *At least I was given the easy one.* As though she had been a piece of luck. As though the great mercy of Eleanor, in the wreckage of that year, had been that she required nothing, asked nothing, took up no more room than a saucepan and a wooden spoon, made no peep — as though she had earned, at nine years old, the right to be kept, by being a child who could be relied upon never to need keeping.

"There's that face," said Diana. "What's that face."

"Nothing." Eleanor cut the cake. The sponge gave under the knife, soft, sweet, slightly too much. "It's a lovely cake, Mum."

"Eleanor." And the brightness was gone now; this was the other voice, the rare one, the one that used her whole name, the one that had told her, at nine, that they would be all right, the two of them, and had been believed. Diana reached out and took Eleanor's wrist, the hand with the cake fork, and held it, and her grip was a pianist's grip still, light and exact, knowing precisely how much pressure a thing would take. "You'd tell me. If you weren't all right. You'd tell me."

And here was the choice — Eleanor felt it arrive, the way she was beginning, this year, to feel them arrive, the small fork in the afternoon, *tell her* or *spare her,* the truth or the comfort — and she did what she had done at nine and had been doing ever since, she chose the comfort, she gave the easy yes, because her mother was sixty-six and forgetting how to count the beats and did not need, on a Tuesday, to carry her daughter as well as a doubled cake; and Eleanor smiled and turned her wrist gently in her mother's grip until she was holding her mother's hand instead of being held, and said, "Of course I would. I'm fine, Mum. I'm completely fine. Eat your cake."

Diana searched her face a moment longer. Then she let it go, let the whole thing go, the way she let more go every month now, and picked up her fork, and the afternoon recovered itself, came back up under the stumble, and they ate the cake, one of the two cakes, in the kitchen where the light was already going.

Afterward, while Eleanor washed the two good plates, Diana went back to the front room and sat down at the piano, not to play, exactly — she put her hands on the keys and left them there, the way some people put their hands on a sleeping animal, for the warmth of the contact and not to wake it — and Eleanor, drying her hands, came and stood behind her, and looked over her mother's shoulder at the keys, the worn ivories, the chipped C, the famous flat D.

"Sit," said Diana. "Come on. Right hand. You still remember the right hand, I'd put money on it; the hands keep things the head's let go of. That's the great secret of it, you know, the thing I never managed to teach a single one of those children who came through this room, that you don't play with your mind, the mind's a liability, the mind gets stage fright and counts wrong and remembers the time it went badly — you play with your hands, and your hands are honest, your hands are like that D, they tell you the truth about where you actually are." She shifted along the stool to make room, the old room, the nine-year-old's room. "Go on. Humour your mother. It's her birthday."

"It's my birthday, Mum."

"Is it?" The flat moment, the dropped stitch — and then, sailing on over it without missing the beat, because the hands keep going even when the head forgets where it is: "Well, then, humour your mother on your birthday, which is even better, there's no one in the world you owe more."

So Eleanor sat, on the half of the stool that had been hers since she was small, and put her right hand on the keys, her hand that had never decided anything, and found — Diana was right, the maddening woman was always right about this one thing — that it remembered. The thumb knew where to fall. The first three notes, the pause, the next three, the slow patient phrase, and her mother's left hand came up underneath it, walking, holding it up, the way it had held up the right hand of every piece in this room for forty years, and they played the Gymnopédie together, badly, the daughter's tentative tune and the mother's sure low chords, and arrived together at the flat D, the small sweet wound, the honest note, and went on past it, and Eleanor felt her eyes sting and was glad her mother was looking at the keys.

"Your father couldn't do this," Diana said, conversationally, eyes on their four hands. "Couldn't carry a tune in a bucket, Patrick, couldn't sit still long enough to learn. I used to play and he'd be up, wandering, out to the garden, off down the pub, off — " a small dry laugh, " — off, in the end, altogether. Some people are made of staying and some people are made of leaving, and you can't teach it, it's like the right hand, it's in the body before it's anywhere else. I always thought — " She stopped. Her hands stopped. For a moment something moved behind her face, some current under the courteous surface, a thing she knew and had perhaps decided, long ago, never to say, or had said once and forgotten saying. "I always thought I'd done the right thing," she said, more quietly, "letting your hands learn to stay. You were so good at it. You stayed and stayed. You stayed when I — " and then the current went under again, and the surface came back up smooth, and Diana lifted her hands and patted Eleanor's knee, brisk, finished. "Listen to me. Maudlin, on your birthday. Pay no attention. Play it once more on your own, the right hand, so I know it's safe in there for when I — so I know you've got it. Then you'll want to be getting back to your nice Adam."

And Eleanor played it once more, the right hand alone, the tune without the chords to hold it up, thin and true in the going light, while her mother sat beside her with her own honest hands at last in her lap and listened to her daughter carry, unaccompanied, the only thing she had ever insisted on passing down — and it was, by every external measure, and by one or two of the internal ones Eleanor did not yet have the courage to measure with, lovely.

## ◆ v. The restaurant, and the other restaurant

*Eleanor*

She walked down off the hill afterward into the early dark, the way she had walked down it ten thousand times, the harbour opening up below her between the rooftops with its scatter of lights, and she carried the day down with her the way you carry a tray, carefully, level, so that nothing slops over the edge: the two cakes, the doubled shells in the caddy, the cardigan on the newel post, her mother's hands going still in her lap, *you stayed when I —* and the sentence her mother had not finished, and Priya's hand across the marking, *I have no idea what you want,* and the grey corridor feeling, and the safe pair of hands, and the kingfisher mug rinsed and empty on the rack at home. She did not examine any of it. She had a gift, the obverse of her mother's gift, the one Diana had passed down without ever meaning to alongside the right hand of the Gymnopédie: she could carry an enormous amount and never spill, never set it down, never look at what was on the tray. It was, she sometimes thought, the most marketable skill she had. Crane & Soder should have put it on the website. *Eleanor Hale, RIBA. A safe pair of hands. Will carry anything. Will never ask what it weighs.*

Her phone buzzed on the way down. Adam: *Booked the usual place, 8. End table. Wear the green one? x.* The end table. The green one — the dress he liked, the dress he had once said made her look like someone he'd have been too shy to talk to, which was, in Adam's grammar, an extravagant thing to say. She knew, reading it, standing on the steep pavement with the lit harbour below her and the cold coming up off the water, exactly what the end table and the green dress meant, knew it the way she knew where the load-bearing wall was, and she felt the old machinery start up in her, smooth and quiet, the readiness, the warming of the seat, the yes laid out ready like cutlery — and underneath the machinery, very far down, under the tray she never set down, something turned over in its sleep and did not wake, not yet.

*Lovely,* she typed back. *See you there. x.* Easy. The easiest thing in the world.

It was, though she had no way of knowing it, the last evening of her life in which the yes would be the easiest thing in the world; from tomorrow morning, for three hundred and sixty-five days, it would be the hardest; and she stood for a moment at the bottom of the hill where it met the harbour road and looked out at the water before she crossed, because the tide was going out, she could smell it, the riverbed coming up, the underneath of things, and she had always, all her life, stopped to look at the water going out, at the boats beginning to lean, at the way the harbour gave back everything it held, slowly, completely, and would fill again by morning as though it had never let anything go.

Adam booked the Thai place on the harbour, the one with the tables along the water, because it was where they had gone on their first proper date and he was a man who kept the anniversaries of small institutions, and Eleanor knew, the moment she saw which table — the end one, the private one, the one you ask for — what the evening was going to contain, the way you know from the overture, even if you have never heard the opera, that someone is going to die or marry by the end of it.

She had known, really, for a year. She had known since the jeweller's quarter, since the significant pauses, since *when we're married*; she had simply declined to know it on any particular evening, and now here was the particular evening, declining to be declined. The harbour was very still. The tide was out, all the way out, the way it goes out in Bristol, so that the water draws back off the mud and leaves the boats leaning and the whole basin smells, briefly and not unpleasantly, of the riverbed, of the underneath of things, of all the days the water has covered and will cover again — and across the black water the lights of the far side hung and doubled, the real lights above and the drowned lights below, the city and the city's reflection, two Bristols, one of them upside down and slightly broken on the moving surface, and Eleanor looked at the doubled lights and thought, with no idea how prophetic the thought was, that she had always preferred the reflection. It asked nothing of you. You could look at the reflection of a life all evening and never have to live in it.

They ordered. They talked, easily, about her mother, about the two cakes, which Eleanor offered up as comedy and Adam received as comedy and underneath which they both felt the cold draught of the thing neither of them was saying, *your mother is going,* the way you feel cold under a door. They talked about Greenbank, about Cora, about Priya's eldest, who had announced at school that his mother was a doctor, which Priya was not, and his father a king, which he was not.

"Do you ever think," Adam said, refilling her glass before her own, the way he always did, anticipating the want a half-beat ahead of it the way she anticipated everyone else's, so that between the two of them no want was ever quite owned by the person who had it, "about what you'd be doing. If you'd done the London thing. Years ago." He said it idly, turning his own glass, an engineer testing a joint he had every reason to believe was sound. "I only ask because Soder mentioned it once, at that Christmas do, that you'd had a big offer and turned it down for — he said for Diana. And you never talk about it, and I realised the other day I've been with you four years and I don't actually know if you mind."

And there it was again, the small fork in the evening, *tell him* or *spare him,* and Eleanor felt the half-second arrive, and looked at this kind man who had asked her, gently, on the very evening he meant to marry her, the one question whose true answer she had spent eight years declining to give even to herself — *yes, I mind, I have a whole locked flat of minding, eleven floors up, with the river in all the windows* — and she did what she did. "I'd be exactly the same," she said, lightly, "just with worse coffee and a longer commute. There's no version of me out there having a better time, if that's what you're worried about." She smiled, and let her hand drift to his on the cloth, and felt him relax under it, the joint holding, the structure sound, and she believed, in the moment she said it, that it was even roughly true, because that was the other thing she was good at, believing her own easy answers for exactly as long as it took to make the room comfortable. "Why. Are you about to whisk me off to London?"

"God, no," said Adam, with feeling, and they both laughed, and the danger passed back under the door, and outside the black water held the lights, and held them, and held them.

The food came. The candle on the table guttered and Adam shielded it, automatically, with his cupped hand, the engineer protecting the small flame from the moving air, and Eleanor watched his hand around the light and felt a great wave of tenderness for him, real, true, the truest thing all day except possibly the cake, tenderness and gratitude and the particular grief that lives one inch under both, and she thought: *I do love him. That was never the question. I have never once been able to find the question.*

And then the plates were cleared, and Adam wiped his mouth, and set down his napkin, and did not reach for the dessert menu, and Eleanor's whole body, which had been managing the evening from the surface, went quiet and attentive all the way down, the way a room goes quiet when the metronome stops.

"Nell," he said.

"Adam." She heard her own voice do it — the half-second — heard the readiness in it, the yes already loaded, the yes that had been loaded for a year, for longer than a year, the yes she had been keeping warm the way you keep a seat, *go on,* her whole self leaning a half-beat ahead into the thing he had not yet asked, so that she could say it the instant the question finished, so that he would never, not for one second, have to watch her decide.

He took the box out of his pocket. He was nervous; she loved him for being nervous; his careful hands were not quite steady on the small hinged lid. "I've been trying to do this for about eleven months," he said, "and I keep — there's never a — I wanted it to be perfect and I've realised the reason I keep waiting for it to be perfect is that I'm scared, and the thing I'm scared of isn't that you'll say no." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and he smiled, this kind, steady, safe man who checked whether other people's buildings would stand up. "It's that I already know you'll say yes. And I've been lying here at night wondering whether you'd say yes to anyone. Whether I'm — whether it's me, or whether it's just that I asked." He opened the box. The ring was exactly right, which is to say it was exactly what she would have chosen, which is to say it was exactly what he had decided she would have chosen, and it caught the candle and the doubled harbour lights and threw them, small and bright, onto the white cloth. "So I'm not going to do a speech. I'm just going to ask you, and I'm going to watch your face when I do, and I want you — Nell, I want you, for once, just once, to actually—"

"Eleanor," he said. "Will you marry me?"

And she opened her mouth to say the yes, the warm loaded easy yes, the yes that cost nothing and would have made the whole restaurant turn and the whole rest of her life fall smoothly into the shape it had been waiting to fall into —

— and the candle went out.

Not the candle. Everything. The restaurant drew back off the edges of her vision the way the tide draws back off the mud, all at once and silently, leaving the leaning boats and the smell of the underneath, and for a length of time she could not afterward measure — a half-second, the half-second, the one she had been living in her whole life, dilated now into a country she could walk around in — Eleanor Hale was somewhere else, in a present tense, in a life that was not hers, that was waiting for her, fully built, with all its lights on.

*She crosses the wet concourse without looking up.*

It is Waterloo, or it is somewhere with the bones of Waterloo, a great vaulted shed full of weather and announcements, and it is raining the way it rains on London termini, sideways, importantly, and Ellis does not look up, because looking up is what people do when they are not sure they are wanted, and Ellis cured herself of that years ago, the way you cure meat, with salt and time and the willingness to let a part of yourself go hard. She has a coat that cost more than Eleanor's first car and she wears it the way you wear armour you have stopped noticing the weight of. Her hands are cold. Her hands are always cold now; the circulation, the doctor says, nothing to worry about, but the truth is the cold suits her, the truth is she has come to think of warm hands as a kind of incompetence, the soft damp hands of people who need to be liked, and Ellis does not need to be liked. Ellis needs to be right, and she is, reliably, expensively right, and the city has paid her for nine straight years to be right about the things it is building in its own image.

She is forty — no. She is thirty-four. It is the same night, Eleanor understands, with the lurching double vision of the dreamer who knows she is dreaming; it is the same birthday, the same October, and Ellis is thirty-four too, and Ellis took the job. That is the whole of it, the single moved wall. Eight years ago a man named — the name will not come, it does not matter, it never mattered — a man rang from a practice in London and offered Eleanor a place on a team designing a tower that would actually get built, the real thing, the thing the magazine photograph had promised, and Eleanor had said, *I can't, my mother's not well, I can't leave Bristol just now,* and had stayed, and the not-well had turned out to be a gallstone, resolved in a fortnight, and she had stayed anyway, because by then there was the firm, and there was a man, and there were the open doors, and to walk through the London one would have meant closing all the others.

Ellis said yes.

*Ellis is never kept and she keeps no one,* Eleanor thinks, in the present tense, from inside Ellis's cold hands, and it is not a sad thought, that is the terrible thing, it is the proudest thought Eleanor has ever had. The flat is on the eleventh floor and the river is in all the windows and there is no one in it and that is not loneliness, Ellis would want it understood, that is *space,* that is the thing Eleanor gave away by the cupful all her life and Ellis has hoarded into a single clear volume, an entire flat full of uninterrupted self, no one's tea going cold on any surface but her own, and her own does not go cold because Ellis, when she makes a cup of tea, drinks it, standing at the glass, watching the lights of the far bank double on the black water, eleven floors up, having decided.

There is an award on the shelf. There are several. There is a photograph of a building she designed, a real building, a building with an idea in it that no one value-engineered out because Ellis does not let them, because Ellis has made herself into the kind of woman they cannot afford to overrule, and Eleanor looks at the photograph from inside the body of the woman who built it and feels the want rise in her like water coming back over the mud, the old want, the thirty-one-year-old want, the want she trained herself out of — *this, I could have had this, this was always mine, I gave it away for a gallstone and a propped-open door.*

There had been a dinner tonight. That is why the coat, why the concourse; Ellis is coming home, not going out, it is gone eleven and she is coming home from a dinner at which a man whose name is on a third of the cranes between here and Vauxhall stood up and said, into a microphone, that Ellis Hale designed not buildings but arguments, that she had done more to change the skyline of this city than any woman of her generation, and three hundred people had applauded, and Ellis had stood at the lectern in the good light with the heavy glass thing they had given her and looked out over the applause and felt — this is the part Eleanor cannot get out of, the part that closes around her like cold water — felt almost nothing. A flicker. The clean small satisfaction of a line that holds. And then the wondering, even as she thanked them, even as she did the modest devastating smile she has trademarked, who she would tell. The genuine practical blankness of it: who, when she got in, gone eleven, would she ring. The assistant had her car. The practice had her diary. There is a man she sees, has seen for two years, a barrister with his own flat and his own blankness, and they suit each other the way two well-run countries suit each other, with treaties and respect and separate currencies and no intention on either side of ever being invaded; she had not rung him from the car and he had not rung her, and this is not a sadness, Ellis would want it understood. This is *efficiency.* This is what you get to keep when you stop spending yourself by the cupful on the comfort of other people. She has, at thirty-four, the cleanest accounts of anyone she knows. Nothing owing. Nothing owed.

In the car, coming back, the phone had lit on the seat beside her, and she had looked at it, and it was a Bristol number, the code she still knows the way you know a scar, and she had let it ring out, because it was gone eleven and because the only people who ring from Bristol after eleven are ringing about her mother, and Ellis has, over eight expensive years, built a wall around the subject of her mother as load-bearing as anything she has ever drawn, a wall that says: *I made my choice, I made it cleanly, I do not get to keep both the choice and the guilt, and so I will keep the choice.* Diana is in a home now, a good one, the best one, Ellis pays for the best one and visits at Christmas and at Easter and stands in the doorway of a pastel room being received by a courteous stranger who sometimes places her and more often does not, and Ellis has decided — this is the kind of thing Ellis decides — that there is a mercy in being the daughter who is mostly not there, because the not-there daughter is not watched declining, the not-there daughter stays, in whatever is left of Diana's mind, a fixed bright thing, *my Eleanor, who did so well, who went to London,* preserved at her best like a building photographed the week it opened, before the weather, before the wandering paths got value-engineered out, before the corridors gave up. The phone had stopped. It would be a voicemail from the home, or from a sister Ellis does not have, a Bristol sister who stayed, who is not real, whom Eleanor can feel hovering at the edge of the vision like a room the architect left off the plan — and Ellis had put the phone in her bag, face down, and watched London go by in the rain, and not rung back, because there is nothing to be done at eleven at night that cannot be done, more efficiently, in the morning, by someone who is paid to do it.

That is the flat. That is the view. That is the award, and the dinner, and the clean accounts, and the phone face down in the bag, and Ellis stands in the middle of all of it, in the uninterrupted volume of herself, the thing Eleanor gave away by the cupful and Ellis hoarded into a single clear space, and she does the thing she does every night when she gets in, which is to walk to the window and look at the lights of the far bank doubling on the black water, the city and its drowned reflection, and decide, again, that she would not change it. She decides it the way you tongue a tooth to check it still hurts. She would not change it. She is the most successful version of herself that was ever available and she would not, she will not, change one single —

and Ellis turns from the window, and crosses the empty beautiful flat, and stops.

Because she is being watched.

She knows it the way you know it, the prickle, the wrongness; she turns, and there is the glass, the black window with the doubled city in it, and in the glass Ellis sees her own reflection looking back, which is ordinary, except that the reflection is not quite doing what she is doing. The reflection is looking *at her.* The reflection's cold expensive face is wearing, very faintly, an expression Ellis does not recognise as her own, an expression of — what. Recognition. Hunger. The reflection in the black glass tilts its head a half-second before Ellis tilts hers, the way the yes used to arrive a half-second early, and it smiles, and it is Eleanor's smile, the easy one, the one that costs nothing, and Ellis — who is not afraid, who cured herself of fear with salt and time — feels, for the first time in eight expensive years, the floor of the eleventh-floor flat go thin beneath her.

*You've been letting things go cold your whole life,* the reflection says, except that it does not say it, the black glass does not move, the words simply arrive in Ellis the way the want arrived, from underneath, from the riverbed, from the part of her she salted and let go hard. *Tea. Mothers. Men named Theo. You let it all go cold and then you tip it down the sink and you call the empty cup a life.* The reflection's easy smile does not waver. *I don't let things go cold. I drink them. I decided. Let me in and you'll never have to stand at a window deciding again — I'll do all of it, every yes, every no, I'll be so sure you'll never feel the half-second again. Just open the door. You're so good at opening doors.*

And Ellis — proud, cold, certain Ellis, who would not change one single thing — lifts one cold hand toward the glass, helplessly, the way you reach for the thing you have wanted so long you have forgotten it is dangerous, and the reflection lifts its hand to meet her, warm to her cold, and where their fingers touch, on the cold black surface, on the drowned doubled lights of a city that is not Bristol —

---

"Nell?"

Adam's voice, far off, then near, then in the room. The restaurant came back the way the tide comes back, not all at once, a brimming at the edges and then the whole basin full again, the candle (relit; he had relit it, or it had never gone out, she would never know which) and the white cloth and the small bright ring throwing its doubled lights, and Adam's face across the table, open, hopeful, beginning — only just beginning — to be afraid.

"Nell? You went — you completely went. Are you all right?" He had half risen. His hand was reaching across the cloth toward hers, the careful hand that shielded flames. "You're white as a sheet. Say something. I shouldn't have — God, I'm sorry, I've ambushed you, we don't have to—"

"No," Eleanor said. Her own voice. Her own warm hands, on the cloth, one of them very close to the open box. The harbour beyond the glass, and the tide, and the doubled lights, and somewhere eleven floors above a city she had never lived in, a woman with cold hands taking her fingers off a pane of black glass. She looked at the ring. She looked at the man. She felt the yes, the loaded easy yes, sitting in her mouth where it had sat for a year, and she felt, for the first time in her whole accommodating life, the weight of the other thing behind it, the thing the half-second was supposed to hide, the wall she had spent thirty-four years pretending wasn't load-bearing —

and Eleanor Hale, who had never in her life kept anyone waiting, looked down at her own two hands that had never decided anything, and did not, yet, say yes.
