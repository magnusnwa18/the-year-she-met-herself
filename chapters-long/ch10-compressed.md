I made myself decide things, real things, fork-shaped things—*should I keep the flat in Totterdown or give it up; should I ring Theo tonight or let him sleep; should I, even, take Julian's job after all, reverse the whole day*—and I waited, braced, for the white light to rise at the edge of my sight, for the cold hand, for the tilt into someone else's life. And there was only the cottage, and the tide, and the tea going pleasantly cold-then-I-drank-it-hot, and my own tired face in the black glass doing exactly what I did, a half-second late, the way a real reflection does. The engine had stopped. Not jammed—stopped, the way an engine stops when it has done its work and arrived. For a moment I was almost frightened by the quiet of it, the way you can be frightened, after a long pain, by its sudden absence, by not knowing who you are without it. I had been a haunted woman for three hundred and sixty-five days, and the haunting had become, God help me, a kind of company—Ellis at every dark window, the others patient behind her—and now the rooms behind me were empty and lit and ordinary, and I would have to learn, at thirty-five, to be a woman with only one life in her, only one self saying *I*, only the single unrepeatable mortal road under her own two feet. It was the loneliest feeling and the most free, and they were, I understood at last, the same feeling, they had always been the same feeling, and the whole year had been learning to tell that they were not the enemy I'd thought.
